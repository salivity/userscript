How to File a DMCA Takedown for Stolen Userscripts

This article provides a comprehensive guide on the legal mechanisms for filing a Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) takedown notice against unauthorized copies of userscripts. Userscripts—custom JavaScript files used to modify web pages—are protected by copyright law upon creation. Protecting your script involves verifying copyright ownership, locating where the stolen script is hosted, preparing a legally compliant DMCA notice, and submitting it to the appropriate host or platform agent.

Understanding Copyright Protection for Userscripts

Userscripts are written source code, making them original literary works under copyright law. The moment a developer writes and saves a unique script, copyright protection applies automatically.

If someone copies, re-uploads, or monetizes your script without authorization or outside the terms of your software license (such as MIT, GPL, or proprietary licenses), they have committed copyright infringement. The primary legal mechanism to resolve this online in the United States (and under similar international frameworks) is the DMCA Takedown Notice under 17 U.S.C. § 512.

Step 1: Verify License Terms and Infringement

Before filing a takedown notice, ensure that an actual copyright violation has occurred:

  • Open-Source Licenses: If your script uses a permissive license like MIT or GPL, check if the copy compliant with the license. Re-uploading code is often permitted under these licenses if attribution and license terms are preserved. Removing attribution or altering proprietary terms violates the license and voids the user's right to distribute your code.
  • Proprietary/All Rights Reserved: If your script does not include an open-source license, any unauthorized distribution is a violation of copyright law.

Step 2: Identify the Host and Designated DMCA Agent

You must send the DMCA notice to the service provider hosting the stolen userscript, not the individual user who uploaded it. Common hosts include:

  • Script Repositories: Greasy Fork, Sleazy Fork, OpenUserJS, and GitHub.
  • Browser Extension Stores: Chrome Web Store, Firefox Add-ons (if packed into an extension).
  • Web Hosts: Cloudflare, AWS, or specific web hosting providers if hosted on a custom site.

Most platforms list a designated DMCA agent or provide a web portal specifically for copyright complaints (e.g., GitHub's DMCA Takedown Form or Google's Copyright Removal Portal). For independent websites, look for a DMCA page, Terms of Service link, or query the site's WHOIS data for host abuse contacts.

Step 3: Draft the DMCA Takedown Notice

To be legally valid under 17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3), your DMCA notice must contain the following six mandatory elements:

  1. Information Identifying the Copyright Owner: Your full legal name, email address, physical address, and telephone number (or that of your authorized agent).
  2. Identification of the Original Work: The URL or description of your original userscript (e.g., your GitHub repository, original Greasy Fork link, or personal blog).
  3. Identification of the Infringing Work: The exact URL(s) of the stolen script that you want removed or disabled.
  4. Good Faith Statement: A clear statement declaring: "I have a good faith belief that the use of the copyrighted material described above is not authorized by the copyright owner, its agent, or the law."
  5. Accuracy Statement: A statement declaring: "I swear, under penalty of perjury, that the information in the notification is accurate and that I am the copyright owner or am authorized to act on behalf of the owner of an exclusive right that is allegedly infringed."
  6. Signature: A physical or electronic signature (typing your legal name at the bottom fulfills the electronic signature requirement).

Step 4: Submit the Notice and Monitor Response

Send the completed notice via the host's designated web form, DMCA email address, or standard mail.

Upon receiving a valid notice, the host is legally obligated to act "expeditiously" to remove or disable access to the infringing userscript to maintain their safe harbor protection under the law.

Step 5: Handling Counter-Notifications

The alleged infringer has the legal right to file a counter-notification if they believe the content was removed by mistake or misidentification.

If a counter-notification is filed:

  • The host will forward the counter-notice to you.
  • The host will restore the material within 10 to 14 business days unless you provide proof that you have filed a federal court action seeking a court order to restrain the infringer from engaging in infringing activity.
